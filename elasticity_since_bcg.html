The one-line finding

Across 15,128 products and 108,979 product×site×cluster rows, the price elasticities Evidensia would set prices on today are materially the same as the ones BCG delivered in 2025 (revenue-weighted −0.532 → −0.512). The model is stable and reproducible on growing data — the property a pricing model needs to be trusted over time.

No large service changed direction; the small movements that exist are low-revenue or weak-signal noise that settled down with more data (page 3). And critically — we proved we reproduce BCG exactly on the old data before trusting the model on new data (page 4).

What the numbers say

The raw median moving from −0.494 to −0.497 would tempt the conclusion that nothing changed. Looked at closely, that conclusion largely holds: products split almost evenly between slightly stronger (46%) and slightly weaker (48%), and the revenue-weighted net drift is +0.020 — negligible.

No large service changed direction clearly. The five biggest by revenue — Consult, Imaging, Surgery, Internal, Hospitalisation, together over 4.7 bn SEK — are essentially flat. The model's reading of how customers respond to price on the core assortment is the same today as it was for BCG.

Conclusion

Price sensitivity on the core assortment held still for ten months. The movements that exist are small and sit off to the side — the next page shows they are either low-revenue or noise.

03 · The finding

Where movement exists, it is noise or low-revenue — not the core

The products that moved most sort into clear groups. Separating them is what turns a convenient “nothing changed” into a trustworthy one: the strong, revenue-bearing signals stayed put.

Noise · discount

Weak-signal items settled down

The largest raw drift sits in Healthcare (+0.97) — but it is only 34 M SEK and its 2025 values were extreme outliers near the model's bounds. The extra data pulled them back toward sensible levels. This is weak-signal stabilisation, not changed customer behaviour.

SBAS0487 −5.668 → −1.041 (1.9 M)
SBCS0124 −8.537 → −6.915 (8.0 M)

So what: nothing to act on — and fresh data taming extreme estimates rather than creating new ones is a sign of a well-behaved model.

Low-revenue · watch

Two small categories moved cleanly

Accessories became clearly more price-sensitive (−0.19 → −0.50; 92% of items stronger) and Consumables uniformly less so (−0.49 → −0.31). These are the only directionally consistent shifts — but both are small (26–49 M SEK), so the business impact is limited.

Accessories −0.188 → −0.501 (26 M, 92% stronger)
Consumables −0.486 → −0.305 (49 M, 0% stronger)

So what: worth a look when revisiting pricing in those categories; not material to the top line.

Why this separation is the proof of soundness

A model that drifted randomly would scatter its largest movements across high-revenue services with no pattern. Instead the movement sorts cleanly: noise-normalisation confined to tiny, weak-signal items, and clean directional shifts only in small categories. The big revenue-bearing services — Consult, Imaging, Surgery, Internal, Hospitalisation — stayed put. That is exactly how a trustworthy elasticity model should behave on fresh data: the signals that matter hold, and the only thing that really moves is the noise getting quieter.

Triangulated conclusion

The fresh-data model is sound: stable where it matters, self-correcting where it was weak, with no large service shifting direction. Prices can be set on these numbers — with a light watch on Accessories and Consumables.

Method & reservations

Grain: product × site × cluster, blended through a 7-level fallback weave. Elasticity = log-log price coefficient (negative = price-sensitive). Drift = today − 2025, revenue-weighted for decision impact. Weak-signal tail items move most and are discarded before any price decision. Stability over time is a reproduction claim, not a causal price-setting claim. Capital/impact figures are order-of-magnitude.
